Merchant Center misrepresentation on WooCommerce: what reviewers check (2026)

A misrepresentation suspension arrives as a short email and one line in Merchant Center. It rarely names the page or the sentence that caused it. Most guides that try to fill the gap are written for Shopify, where the theme, the policy pages and the product feed come from one vendor. A WooCommerce store is assembled from WordPress, WooCommerce, a theme, a feed plugin and whatever the last developer left behind, so there are more places for two pages to disagree.

Below is what a reviewer can see on a WooCommerce store, in the order we'd check it, with the screen where each fix lives.

What the policy actually asks

Google's misrepresentation policy comes down to one question: would a shopper be misled about who you are, what you sell or what it costs? It names false identity or contact details, hiding shipping and return costs, offers you can't fulfil, and unreliable claims. It also says the most serious cases can lead to suspension without prior warning.

On a WooCommerce store, those questions map to a handful of things a reviewer can open in a browser.

1. Business identity and contact details

The reviewer wants to know who they would be buying from and whether they could reach them. Google's Merchant Center guidelines ask for contact details that are easy to find: a form, an email address, a phone number or a business social profile.

On WooCommerce, the business name and address live in several places that don't update each other:

  • WooCommerce → Settings → General → Store Address, which feeds emails and some themes.
  • The Contact page, usually a block or form plugin with its own text.
  • The footer, set in the theme's customiser or a footer widget.
  • The site's Organization structured data, often written by an SEO plugin from its own settings screen.
  • Merchant Center's business information: name, address, phone and website.

Pick one legal name, one trading name, one phone in international format and one email at your own domain. Then make all five agree. Registered in one country and selling to another? Say so on the Contact page.

2. The refund and returns policy, and WooCommerce's sample page

WooCommerce creates a sample page called "Refund and Returns Policy" at /refund_returns/. It's template text with a generic return window. If that page is published alongside your real policy, the store now shows two policies that contradict each other, and the reviewer can't tell which one you honour.

Go to Pages → All Pages, search for "Refund", and keep exactly one returns page published. Set the sample to Draft if you don't use it. Some feed plugins show a setup hint that looks for that sample slug. A red hint in a plugin is not Google's review, so don't publish the sample just to turn it green.

Your real policy should state the return window, who pays return postage, how to start a return and when the refund arrives. Then copy the same terms into Merchant Center's return policy settings, with the URL of that page. Google's return policy setup guide expects the website and the settings to match.

3. Shipping page versus shipping settings versus checkout

This is the mismatch that catches stores with otherwise clean pages. Three things have to tell the same story:

  1. Your shipping page: where you ship, how long orders take to leave, how long delivery takes, and what it costs.
  2. WooCommerce → Settings → Shipping: the zones and methods that produce the price at checkout. Also check WooCommerce → Settings → General → Selling location(s) and Shipping location(s).
  3. Merchant Center's shipping services: cost, handling time and transit time for each country you target.

The quickest test is a cart. Add one unit of a product, enter an address in a country you target, and note the shipping price. Do the same for your heaviest product and for a far-away address. If Merchant Center says "free" and checkout charges anything, every listing promised something the store didn't deliver.

If Google for WooCommerce offers to copy shipping from WooCommerce during setup, check the result: a rate added by a fulfilment app won't be in your WooCommerce zones.

4. Terms and privacy describe this store

A reviewer reads the terms for who the contract is with and what you sell. A terms page left over from a different business model ("we sell supplements" on a cosmetics store) contradicts you in your own words.

Set the pages WooCommerce links at checkout in WooCommerce → Settings → Advanced → Page setup (the Terms and conditions field) and the privacy page in Settings → Privacy in WordPress. The privacy page should name the services that actually receive customer data: payments, hosting, analytics, the feed plugin, email and any fulfilment partner.

5. Claim words

Claims are where health-adjacent stores fail most often. Read every product title, short description, product tab and FAQ field for:

  • Medical wording: treat, cure, heal, prevent, or the drug-style disclaimer "not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease" on a product that isn't a supplement or a drug.
  • Promises you can't prove: "#1", "best", "clinically proven" with no study linked, "certified" with no certifier named, or a "guarantee" you couldn't honour.
  • Comparisons to treatments: "Botox-like", "medical-grade", "pharmaceutical-grade".
  • Fake discounts: a crossed-out regular price in WooCommerce that the product never sold at. The misrepresentation policy covers dishonest pricing, and a permanent "sale" is the most common WooCommerce version of it.

Check the places you don't see every day: the site tagline in Settings → General (many themes print it in the footer and use it as the home page meta description), page titles written by an SEO plugin, and old blog posts that the menu doesn't link but the sitemap does. WordPress lists every public URL at /wp-sitemap.xml, or at the sitemap address your SEO plugin uses.

6. Price and availability: page, structured data and feed

WooCommerce prints Product structured data on each product page, including the offer price, currency and stock status. Google for WooCommerce (in Marketing → Google for WooCommerce) sends a separate copy of the same facts to Merchant Center. A reviewer, or Google's own systems, can compare the page, the structured data and the feed.

Typical WooCommerce causes of a mismatch:

  • a scheduled sale that ended, while a page cache still serves the sale price;
  • tax shown differently on the page than in the feed, set under WooCommerce → Settings → Tax;
  • variable products where the feed sends one variant's price and the page shows another;
  • "In stock" in the feed for an item set to out of stock on the product's Inventory tab.

After any change, spot-check three products: the price on the page, in the page source (search for "price") and in Merchant Center.

7. The cart and checkout work for a stranger

Google's guidelines require that shoppers can add items to the cart and complete checkout, with at least one conventional payment method on a secure page. Open the store in a private window and go as far as the payment step.

Things that break this on WooCommerce:

  • WooCommerce → Settings → Site visibility still set to anything other than Live;
  • a Cart or Checkout page missing from WooCommerce → Settings → Advanced → Page setup;
  • checkout that requires an account before purchase, set in WooCommerce → Settings → Accounts & Privacy;
  • a security or maintenance plugin that blocks visitors from some countries, including the one the reviewer may be browsing from.

8. robots.txt lets Google's crawlers in

If Google can't fetch your product pages, it can't check them. Google's product crawl issues page says robots.txt should allow Googlebot and Googlebot-Image to crawl the site. Google's crawler documentation also lists Storebot-Google, whose rules apply across Google Shopping.

Open yourstore.com/robots.txt and look for Disallow: / under any of those user agents, or under *. In WordPress, also check Settings → Reading → Search engine visibility, and any rules an SEO or security plugin adds.

9. Footer and social links

Every link in the footer is part of your identity. A social profile with no name, no description and no link back to your store reads as a business that doesn't exist. A personal profile linked as "our Facebook page" is worse than no link. Fill each profile in or remove the link, and remove it from the Organization structured data too.

Check the footer on a product page too: some themes use a different one there, and that's where a listing lands.

A real example: our partner store

Our partner store is a small WooCommerce skincare shop that sells to the United States. Its Merchant Center account was suspended for misrepresentation in October 2026, and Google's summary pointed at business transparency, policies and contact information. Going through it the way a reviewer would, we found:

  • Merchant Center's only US shipping service said free shipping, while checkout charged a flat $6.63 for one item.
  • The terms page said the store sold dietary supplements. It sells none.
  • Product tabs carried the drug-style disclaimer on cosmetic products.
  • An unnamed "certified" claim sat in the site tagline, so it appeared in the footer and meta description of every page.
  • Old blog posts, outside the menu but listed in the sitemap, used treatment comparisons such as "Botox-like".
  • Supplier copy on some product pages still addressed other brands, and the footer linked an empty social profile.

The WooCommerce sample refund page was already unpublished, which was one thing that didn't need fixing. The fixes went live between 16 and 18 October. At the time of writing, the review hasn't been requested yet. We'll only describe an outcome once Google has made its decision.

Requesting the review, and the cool-down

Request the review only when every item above is fixed or has a plain explanation. The button is on the account issue in Merchant Center. Google's guide to warnings and suspensions says reviews typically take up to 3–7 business days.

The same page explains the cost of asking too early. A suspended account gets a limited number of review requests before a cool-down starts. During the cool-down the review button is disabled and the account stays suspended. After each further unsuccessful review, Google may make the next cool-down longer. You can find the end date under Products → Needs attention → View setup and Policy issues.

So treat the first request as the one that counts. Keep the appeal short and checkable: who you are, what you changed, with a URL and date for each change. Don't claim fixes you haven't made.

FAQ

Can I fix a misrepresentation suspension on WooCommerce without a developer?

Usually, yes. Most fixes are page text, WooCommerce settings (shipping, page setup, site visibility) and Merchant Center settings. Theme templates that add text to every product page sometimes need a developer.

Should I delete WooCommerce's sample Refund and Returns Policy page?

Set it to Draft if you don't use it. What matters is that only one returns policy is public, and that Merchant Center's return policy settings point at that page with the same terms.

Does Google for WooCommerce cause misrepresentation suspensions?

The plugin sends your product data; it doesn't decide the review. But stale prices, wrong stock or shipping settings copied incorrectly during setup can make your listings disagree with your store, and that disagreement is what a reviewer sees.

How long does a Merchant Center review take?

Google's help pages say review requests typically take up to 3–7 business days, and complex cases can take longer.

What happens if my review fails?

After a limited number of attempts, Google starts a cool-down during which you can't request another review, and later cool-downs may be longer. Use the time to recheck everything, not only the item you suspect.

Can anyone promise reinstatement?

No. Google decides every review. Anyone promising reinstatement, or claiming they can contact Google for you, is promising something they don't control.
